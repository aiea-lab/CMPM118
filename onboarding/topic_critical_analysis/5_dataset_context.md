# Task 5: Topic Basics 1 — Categories, Context, and Dataset Documentation

## Objective and prerequisites

Use [Task 4's feminist frameworks](4_feminist_frameworks.md) to analyze how a dataset's purpose, categories, and collection conditions shape what researchers can claim. Practice documentation as a critical research method, then examine its limits. Allow 5–7 hours. No programming, model querying, or new data collection is required.

## 1. Read and develop a concept map

Read Catherine D'Ignazio and Lauren F. Klein (2020), [*Data Feminism*, Chapter 6, “The Numbers Don't Speak for Themselves”](https://data-feminism.mitpress.mit.edu/pub/czq9dfs5/release/3), and Timnit Gebru et al. (2021), [*Datasheets for Datasets*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09010), including its proposed questions and discussion.

**Predict:** If every field in a dataset has a precise definition, what could still be missing from its documentation? After reading, revise your answer with a passage from each text.

Create a concept map connecting categories, collection context, intended use, missingness, and institutional authority. Explain three connections in 200–250 words with page or section references. Connect one to intersectionality or situated knowledge from Task 4. Do not treat documenting a decision as demonstrating that the decision was justified.

## 2. Analyze a bounded dataset proposal

Use the following fictional artifact, extending the scholarship case from Task 4. All claims in this box are stipulated features of the exercise, not findings about a real university:

> A university proposes a dataset of scholarship applications from the past five years. Each row is an application, so a student may appear more than once. Columns include GPA, full-time enrollment history, unpaid extracurricular hours, gender recorded as woman/man/other/blank, broad racial categories, and the historical award decision. Applications submitted after the deadline are absent. Documentation does not say how categories were assigned, whether applicants could correct records, or why the deadline was chosen. The team proposes using past award decisions as ground truth for a future shortlist. It says the dataset can be shared with outside researchers after names are removed.

### Exercise: Draft a critical datasheet

Select 12 relevant questions from *Datasheets for Datasets*, spanning motivation, composition, collection, preprocessing, use, distribution, and maintenance. Cite where you selected them and create a table with these columns:

| Documentation question | Known from proposal | Unknown or unsupported | Evidence needed | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Use “not specified” when evidence is absent. Do not invent consent, collection practices, demographic composition, or outcomes. Include the unit of analysis, repeated applications, exclusions, provenance of labels, and proposed data sharing. Explain why removing names alone does not settle the sharing question; identify the evidence and governance review still needed without making a legal determination.

## 3. Analyze categories and labels

Write 400–500 words analyzing two design choices: the recorded demographic categories and the use of historical awards as ground truth. Apply intersectionality to one choice and situated knowledge to the other. Cite at least two passages from the required Task 5 readings and one from Task 4 across the analysis.

Distinguish an observation supported by the proposal, a plausible concern, and a question requiring evidence. Explain how apparently missing records could reflect the application process rather than individual characteristics. Avoid treating a demographic label as a reliable proxy for lived experience or assuming the historical award decision measures merit.

## 4. Revise one component and reassess

Choose **one** change: redefine the target label, revise category governance, or include a new source of evidence about the application process. Describe the original and revised versions, predict what conclusions could change, and identify one concern the revision would leave unresolved. Create a before/after table with at least three consequences and write a 200–250 word interpretation.

This is a conceptual comparison, not an experiment with observed outcomes. Specify who should have decision authority and how a disagreement could be recorded or challenged. Discuss when a well-documented dataset might still be inappropriate to build or use.

## 5. Transfer and reflection

Write a 150–200 word memo explaining how you would document the evidence and uncertainties for a future lab project. Propose one research question that could be answered with existing public documentation and one that would require a different method. You do not need to contact participants. Include two passage-based discussion questions and one way the readings changed your initial prediction.

## Deliverable and evaluation

Upload one PDF or Markdown document containing predictions, concept map, datasheet table, category analysis, revision comparison, memo, discussion questions, and references to Canvas under Deliverable 5. Aim for 1,200–1,600 words excluding tables and references.

Assessment considers accurate reading, traceable evidence, analytical use of frameworks, explicit uncertainty, and the limits of documentation. Personal disclosure and agreement with the instructor are not required. If a reading is inaccessible, request a course copy or locate the same text through the library and record its edition. Task 6 retains the existing model-based project activities; completing this task does not require carrying out an audit or collecting new student records.
