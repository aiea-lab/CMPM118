# Task 4: Framework Basics — Feminist Frameworks for Technology Analysis

## Objective and prerequisites

Build on [Task 3](3_project_onboarding.md) by practicing two analytical frameworks: intersectionality and situated knowledge. Here, a framework is a set of concepts that guides what questions you ask, what counts as evidence, and how you interpret a problem. Your goal is to apply concepts carefully, compare what they reveal, and revise an analysis. No coding, model queries, or compute setup is required. Allow 5–7 hours.

## 1. Read the frameworks

Read both required texts. Start with a first pass for the main argument, then revisit passages connected to the questions below. Maintain a glossary of unfamiliar terms with your own tentative definitions and page references.

1. Kimberlé Crenshaw (1989), [“Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex: A Black Feminist Critique of Antidiscrimination Doctrine, Feminist Theory and Antiracist Politics”](https://chicagounbound.uchicago.edu/uclf/vol1989/iss1/8/), *University of Chicago Legal Forum*, Article 8, pp. 139–167. Read the full article. Explain how its legal examples motivate the critique of single-axis analysis. Avoid reducing intersectionality to a checklist of demographic attributes.
2. Catherine D'Ignazio and Lauren F. Klein (2020), [*Data Feminism*, Chapter 3, “On Rational, Scientific, Objective Viewpoints from Mythical, Imaginary, Impossible Standpoints”](https://data-feminism.mitpress.mit.edu/pub/5evfe9yd/release/5). Read the full chapter. Explain how the chapter approaches knowledge, embodiment, and claims to objectivity. Identify what evidence supports your interpretation.

**Optional primary-source extension:** Donna Haraway (1988), [“Situated Knowledges: The Science Question in Feminism and the Privilege of Partial Perspective”](https://www.jstor.org/stable/3178066), *Feminist Studies*, 14(3), 575–599. Library access may be needed. The two required readings are openly available; this extension is not required for completion.

**Predict before reading:** Does treating everyone with the same rule guarantee a fair result? Does acknowledging a researcher's standpoint make careful evidence less necessary? Write an initial answer to each, then revise it after reading with a passage reference.

### Exercise 1: Framework reference cards

Create one card per required framework, roughly 200–250 words each. Include its central question, two key concepts, a specific example from the reading, two questions you could ask of a technology project, and a limit or common misapplication. Cite at least two passages per card by printed page or section/paragraph. Separate the author's argument from your interpretation.

## 2. Practice on a worked case

Use this fictional proposal throughout the task:

> A university plans an automated scholarship shortlist. Its score combines GPA, uninterrupted full-time enrollment, and unpaid extracurricular participation. The team argues these are objective records of merit. Its dashboard reports selection rates for women versus men and separately for broad racial categories. Students may appeal through a web form reviewed by the same team. The proposal contains no information about disability, caring responsibilities, paid employment, or how the gender categories were recorded.

A useful analysis starts with an observable feature and develops a question. For example, uninterrupted enrollment is an explicit criterion; a question is how the rule treats students with different responsibilities. The proposal alone does not establish which students were affected or how often. Keep the criterion, your hypothesis, and the evidence needed to investigate it separate.

### Exercise 2: Apply the frameworks separately

Write two analyses of the same proposal, 250–300 words each:

- **Intersectionality:** Identify what the separately reported group averages could conceal. Use Crenshaw's argument to explain why changing the dashboard alone might leave the definition of merit unexamined. Identify evidence needed to investigate a specific institutional mechanism; do not infer someone's circumstances from identity labels.
- **Situated knowledge:** Examine who defined merit, whose experiences informed the criteria, and whose knowledge could challenge them. Explain what makes a claim accountable to evidence and context. Avoid treating every perspective as equally supported or assuming any group has one uniform standpoint.

For each analysis, cite at least two passages from its required reading, propose a concrete revision, and explain how you would assess whether that revision helps. Do not invent outcomes for this fictional case.

## 3. Compare and revise

### Exercise 3: An analytical audit trail

Create a table with at least four rows and these columns:

| Proposal feature | Framework/concept | Initial interpretation | Evidence needed | Proposed change | Remaining limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Then change **one feature** of the proposal: replace the dashboard with intersectional reporting, or replace the appeal process with a paid student/community review panel with stated decision authority. Hold the other features fixed. Predict which part of your critique should change and which should remain. Write a 200–250 word reassessment. Treat this as a conceptual comparison, not an empirical experiment: no actual outcomes have been observed.

Return briefly to Tuck and Yang from Task 3. Explain in 100–150 words why these feminist analyses and revisions do not automatically establish decolonization. Identify a difference in the questions being asked rather than treating the readings as competing labels for the same reform.

## 4. Transfer to a research question

### Exercise 4

Draft a 150–200 word research memo proposing one question for a future lab project. Specify the framework, the claim you would examine, two forms of evidence you would need, who should help define or contest the interpretation, and one possible finding that would lead you to revise your initial view. You do not need to collect data or contact participants.

Include two discussion questions tied to particular passages. Personal disclosure is not required. Disagreement with an author is welcome when you reconstruct the argument accurately and support your response.
