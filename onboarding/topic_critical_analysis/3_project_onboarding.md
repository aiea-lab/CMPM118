# Task 3: Critical Analysis Crash Course

This tutorial introduces close reading, comparison, and evidence-based critique. You will examine how power shapes research questions, categories, and institutions, then apply those perspectives to a technology claim. No programming, model execution, API access, or compute setup is required for this task.

## How to use this tutorial

Work through the sections in order. Keep a reading journal with page or section references. Before reading, answer the **Predict** question; afterward, revisit it. Predictions are checked for completion, not agreement with an author. Exercises are assessed for accurate interpretation, textual evidence, and clear reasoning. You may disagree with a reading if you represent its argument fairly and support your response.

Allow approximately 5–7 hours for reading, notes, and writing. Unfamiliar terminology is expected: record questions rather than treating the text as a vocabulary test.

## 1. Close reading: decolonization

**Required reading:** Eve Tuck and K. Wayne Yang (2012), [“Decolonization is not a metaphor”](https://edwebcontent.ed.ac.uk/sites/default/files/atoms/files/tuck_and_yang_2012_decolonization_is_not_a_metaphor.pdf), *Decolonization: Indigeneity, Education & Society*, 1(1), 1–40. Read the full essay in two sittings, first tracing the main argument and then revisiting difficult passages.

**Predict:** What do you currently understand “decolonization” to mean? What would make a use of the word metaphorical?

A close reading distinguishes the authors' claim, the evidence or examples supporting it, and your interpretation. For example, when encountering an institutional promise to “decolonize innovation,” first identify what that institution actually promises to change. Then ask whether the essay supports describing those changes with this term. Do not assume that every worthwhile reform shares the same political goals.

### Exercise 1: Build an argument map

- Summarize the central argument in 150–200 words, citing two specific passages by page number.
- Explain the relationship between Indigenous land, sovereignty, and the authors' account of decolonization. Why is a generic commitment to inclusion insufficient to describe that account?
- Choose one example of a “settler move to innocence” discussed in the essay. Explain its role in the argument rather than simply labeling it harmful.
- Identify one passage you found difficult. Offer a tentative interpretation and a question for discussion.
- Revisit your prediction. What changed, and what remains unresolved?

**Self-check:** Have you distinguished the authors' position from your own? Could your summary mistakenly turn decolonization into a synonym for diversity, fairness, or improving a dataset? If so, revise it.

## 2. Reading power through a second perspective

**Required reading:** Catherine D'Ignazio and Lauren F. Klein (2020), [“The Power Chapter,” Chapter 1 of *Data Feminism*](https://data-feminism.mitpress.mit.edu/pub/vi8obxh7/release/4). Read the full chapter, including its examples and figures.

**Predict:** When a dataset omits people or experiences, what questions would you ask beyond “How can we collect more data?”

For each example you read, separate who defines the problem, who creates the categories, and who can challenge the resulting decisions. A category can be technically consistent while still requiring a political or historical explanation.

### Exercise 2: Compare without collapsing

Create a table with one row per reading and these columns: central problem, key concepts, evidence or examples, proposed changes, and a question you still have. Include at least two page or section references per reading across your journal.

Then write 250–350 words comparing the texts. Identify one useful connection and one difference in scope or political commitments. Explain why applying a feminist analysis of data does not, by itself, demonstrate that a project is decolonial. Avoid presenting the two texts as interchangeable positions or forcing them into agreement.

**Optional extension:** Read the introduction to Ruha Benjamin's (2019) [*Race After Technology: Abolitionist Tools for the New Jim Code*](https://www.wiley-vch.de/en/areas-interest/humanities-social-sciences/sociology-12so/population-demography-12sog/race-after-technology-978-1-5095-2640-6) through the library or an available course copy. Add a third row to your comparison. The publisher description is a starting point for locating the book, not a substitute for reading it.

## 3. Practice: analyze a claim

Consider this fictional university announcement:

> Our new AI advising system will decolonize student support by giving every student the same automated recommendations. We will train it on historical graduation data, measure success by graduation rates, and invite students to complete a satisfaction survey after launch.

**Predict:** Which claim would be easiest to measure? Which would require historical and institutional evidence beyond the model's performance?

### Exercise 3: Annotate the announcement

Write 400–500 words that:

1. Separate claims about performance, equal treatment, and decolonization.
2. Identify assumptions about students, historical records, and institutional authority. Explain whose perspectives would be needed to investigate them.
3. Use a specific passage from each required reading to analyze the announcement. Explain what each passage helps you see and the limits of that application.
4. Identify what the announcement does not establish. Do not invent the university's history or treat an absence of evidence as proof of a particular harm.
5. Propose two concrete changes to how the project is governed or evaluated, including a way for affected students to challenge a decision. Explain what these changes could accomplish and why they do not establish decolonization on their own.

**Self-check:** Did you analyze who sets the goals, rather than only suggesting a more accurate model? Are your factual claims supported or clearly marked as questions?

## 4. Discussion and reflection

### Exercise 4

Write 150–200 words reflecting on one assumption you revised and one question you would bring to the lab. Prepare two discussion questions that invite interpretation or disagreement and include the passages that motivated them. Personal disclosure is not required; focus on the readings and institutional practices.

## Submission

Upload one PDF or Markdown document to Canvas containing your predictions and revisions, argument map, comparison table and response, case analysis, reflection, discussion questions, and references. Aim for roughly 1,200–1,600 words total, excluding the table and references; short journal notes may accompany the written exercises.

Completion requires all four exercises. Strong work represents each author accurately, uses page or section citations, distinguishes evidence from inference, and develops a supported argument. Agreement with the authors or instructor is not an assessment criterion.
